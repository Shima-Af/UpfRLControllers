\begin{abstract}
The distribution of 5G User Plane Functions (UPFs) across Mobile Edge Computing (MEC) sites improves service responsiveness but increases the energy footprint of the mobile data plane. Since edge traffic varies substantially across sites and over time, static provisioning often keeps high-performance UPF realizations active even when lower-power alternatives would be sufficient. This paper studies energy-aware UPF orchestration as a cooperative runtime-control problem in which multiple heterogeneous MEC clusters must select between a high-throughput DPDK-based UPF and a lower-power user-space UPF while preserving Quality of Service (QoS) and accounting for switching overheads. We evaluate this problem using a measurement-grounded digital twin calibrated from a physical UPF testbed, incorporating empirical power measurements, QoS surrogate models, and measured activation delays. Within this environment, we compare three reinforcement-learning control architectures: MAPPO with a shared actor and centralized critic, independent per-site PPO agents, and a centralized PPO controller. Results over a held-out test period show that MAPPO consistently outperforms the strongest hysteresis baseline and both PPO alternatives, with statistically significant gains over independent learning. The improvement is most visible in low-load, data-poor clusters, where shared policies transfer useful operating behavior across sites. These findings show that coordination structure is a key factor in energy-efficient UPF orchestration.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Mobile networks are being pushed toward a more distributed
architecture. To meet latency requirements and support data-intensive edge
services, operators increasingly deploy core-network functions closer to radio
access sites and end users. The User Plane Function (UPF) is central in this
architecture because it carries packet forwarding, traffic steering, and QoS
enforcement in the 5G Core. As UPFs are replicated across Mobile Edge Computing
(MEC) sites, their aggregate energy consumption becomes an important operational
cost and a relevant sustainability concern.

The difficulty is that edge traffic is neither uniform nor constant. Some MEC
clusters serve dense and persistent demand, while others spend long periods at
low load and experience only short peaks. Provisioning every site with a
high-throughput data-plane realization is therefore safe but inefficient. It
keeps resources active for peak conditions that occur only occasionally. At the
same time, using an energy-efficient realization everywhere is unsafe, because it
may violate latency or packet-loss constraints when load increases. Energy-aware
UPF orchestration must therefore make repeated runtime decisions that balance
three competing requirements: reduce power at low load, preserve QoS at high
load, and avoid excessive switching between realizations.

This paper focuses on a concrete realization-selection problem. Each MEC site
can operate either a DPDK-based kernel-bypass UPF or a user-space UPF. The DPDK
realization provides robust high-load performance but maintains a comparatively
high idle-power floor because CPU cores remain actively polled. The user-space
realization consumes substantially less energy at low load, but its safe
operating region is narrower. The resulting control problem is simple to state
but difficult to solve in practice. At every 15-minute interval, each site must
select the realization that minimizes energy while keeping QoS violations rare,
using imperfect short-horizon traffic forecasts and paying the measured cost of
changing realization.

A single-site controller can address this problem with thresholds or reinforcement learning. These approaches are useful, but they do not fully
capture the operational setting of a fleet of heterogeneous MEC clusters. In a
fleet, some sites generate abundant training experience around the relevant
switching boundary, whereas low-load sites may rarely observe unsafe states and
may not provide enough local evidence for an independent learner to identify
when the user-space realization is safe. In contrast, a controller trained on the
combined experience of all sites may learn reusable operating rules, such as
when low predicted load justifies the user-space realization, and apply them to
sites with limited local data. This motivates a cooperative multi-agent view of
UPF orchestration.

The main question addressed in this work is whether cooperation across MEC
sites improves energy-aware UPF realization control, and whether the benefit
comes from the coordination architecture itself. To answer this question, we
compare three architectures under identical measurements, traffic traces,
reward definitions, and evaluation protocol. The first is MAPPO, which uses a
shared actor for decentralized per-site decisions and a centralized critic
through centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE). The second is
an independent per-site PPO ensemble, where each MEC cluster learns its own
policy without sharing experience. The third is a centralized PPO policy that
observes the global state and outputs the joint action for all sites. This
comparison separates the effect of policy sharing and centralized value learning
from the broader use of reinforcement learning.

Credible evaluation requires a system model that reflects the actual
energy--performance behavior of UPF realizations. For this reason, the study is
performed on a measurement-grounded digital twin calibrated from a physical
5G-core testbed. The digital twin is calibrated with measured power, QoS, and switching-delay data from both UPF realizations. As a result, switching is not modeled as a fixed artificial penalty. Instead, its cost depends on the realization being activated and on the offered load, reflecting the behavior observed in the measured system. This allows the evaluation environment to capture the interaction between traffic demand, UPF realization, QoS, and energy consumption more realistically.

The paper makes the following contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{A cooperative formulation of energy-aware UPF realization control.}
We formulate runtime selection between DPDK and user-space UPF realizations
across $K=10$ heterogeneous MEC clusters as a cooperative multi-agent
reinforcement-learning problem with binary per-site actions and a shared fleet
objective.

\item \emph{A measurement-grounded evaluation environment.}
We formulate the UPF control problem within a calibrated digital twin derived from physical testbed measurements. The environment combines RAPL-based power measurements collected with Scaphandre, QoS surrogate models for each UPF realization, and empirical activation durations with their associated energy costs. This allows policies to be compared under hardware-grounded energy, QoS, and switching-cost conditions.

\item \emph{A controlled comparison of coordination architectures.}
We compare MAPPO, an IPPO ensemble, and centralized PPO under the same traffic traces, reward formulation, and switching-cost model. The comparison shows that the coordination architecture has a measurable impact on energy-aware UPF orchestration, with MAPPO achieving the best overall performance among the evaluated learning-based controllers.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Energy-aware UPF realization and placement.}
Energy reduction in 5G-core deployments has been studied through UPF placement,
chaining, scaling, and implementation choices. Placement-oriented formulations
optimize where UPFs should be located and how traffic should be routed through
them~\cite{ref:leyva}. Complementary measurement studies show that different
UPF realizations have markedly different energy--performance profiles: DPDK or
VPP-based data planes achieve high packet-processing performance but tend to
keep CPU resources active through polling, while user-space implementations
exhibit stronger load-proportional behavior~\cite{ref:christakis,ref:bolla}.
These observations motivate runtime realization selection, where the system
switches between a high-performance and a low-power implementation according to
offered load.

\subsection{Threshold and hysteresis control.}
A common classical approach is to switch realizations using thresholds around
an energy--performance crossover. Hysteresis improves over a single threshold by
introducing separate upper and lower switching points, thereby reducing
oscillation caused by traffic noise or forecast errors. A single-site study
reported 10.46\% energy savings over static DPDK using polynomial surrogates and
an LSTM-based traffic predictor~\cite{ref:slices}. However, the hysteresis band
must be large enough to absorb forecast uncertainty. When the forecast error is
large relative to the switching threshold, the lower threshold may collapse to
zero, causing the controller to remain in the conservative DPDK mode. In this
paper, hysteresis is therefore treated as a strong deterministic baseline: its
thresholds are derived from the calibrated twin and its band is swept to identify
the best classical operating point.

\subsection{Reinforcement learning for UPF and edge-network control.}
Reinforcement learning has been used to adapt UPF deployment and edge-resource
management decisions under time-varying demand. Bellin et al.~\cite{ref:bellin}
use deep RL to select UPF deployment options at the edge based on measured power
behavior. A related single-site formulation~\cite{ref:comcom} models DPDK/USR
selection as an MDP evaluated through a measurement-grounded twin and reports
energy savings over static DPDK. These works demonstrate that learning-based
control can outperform fixed policies, but they focus on one site or treat UPF
options as independent choices. The present work extends this direction to a
fleet of heterogeneous MEC clusters and asks whether shared learning across
sites improves energy-aware realization selection.

\subsection{Cooperative MARL architectures.}
Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning has been applied to network
slicing, radio-resource allocation, traffic steering, and energy-aware radio
access control~\cite{ref:zhu,ref:cai}. Value-decomposition methods such as VDN
and QMIX decompose a global value into agent-specific terms~\cite{ref:vdn,ref:qmix},
but their assumptions and off-policy training style are not always convenient
for short-horizon operational control with heterogeneous reward scales. MAPPO
has emerged as a robust on-policy actor--critic method for cooperative tasks,
combining decentralized actors with a centralized critic~\cite{ref:mappo}. At
the same time, independent PPO can be highly competitive in some cooperative
settings~\cite{ref:ippo}. This makes architecture-level comparison important.
Coordination may help, but centralized learning can also introduce optimization
difficulties. Our study compares MAPPO, independent per-site PPO, and centralized
PPO under a common UPF-control environment.
\subsection{Measurement-grounded digital twins.}
Digital twins provide a controlled way to evaluate network-management policies
without repeatedly perturbing a live infrastructure~\cite{ref:ndt}. Their value,
however, depends on calibration fidelity. For energy-aware control, the twin
must reproduce not only operating power but also QoS degradation and the
costs of configuration changes. A companion study develops a broader
digital-twin vision for energy-aware UPF orchestration in 6G edge systems
~\cite{ref:costandt}. This paper uses a concrete calibrated instance of that
idea as an evaluation instrument for cooperative MARL.

\section{System Model and Digital Twin}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Deployment model and control loop} 
We consider a mobile operator with $K=10$ MEC clusters serving different geographic regions. Each cluster hosts one UPF service that can be instantiated using either a DPDK-based realization or a user-space realization. The control entity operates in the management-and-orchestration layer and issues lifecycle operations to start or stop the corresponding containers at each site. The central 5G Core control plane remains unchanged. The SMF, AMF, UDM, and PCF continue to operate centrally, while the SMF sends N4/PFCP rules to the active UPF realization at each MEC cluster.{\color{red} [Check the flow with Chiara.]}

At control step $t$, with a 15-minute period, each cluster $i$ selects an action $a_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}$, where $0$ denotes the DPDK realization and $1$ denotes the user-space realization. The joint action across the fleet belongs to $\{0,1\}^{K}$ and therefore grows exponentially with the number of sites. This structure makes a centralized joint policy difficult to train as $K$ increases, while also creating an opportunity for policy sharing across sites. The control environment is implemented as a PettingZoo parallel environment~\cite{ref:pz} that wraps the calibrated digital twin.

\subsection{Digital twin calibration}
For each cluster $i$, time step $t$, offered load $\lambda_{i,t}$, and selected
realization $a_{i,t}$, the digital twin returns operating power
$P^{\mathrm{op}}_{i,t}$, packet delay $d_{i,t}$, packet loss $\ell_{i,t}$,
and a binary safety indicator. The twin is calibrated from a physical testbed
that includes an SD-Core DPDK UPF, an OpenAirInterface user-space UPF, Keysight
LoadCore traffic generation, and RAPL power measurements collected through
Scaphandre. The testbed is profiled over various traffic-load levels, creating the
measurement basis for the per-realization models.

For each realization $m$, regression surrogates $p_m(\rho)$ and $q_m(\rho)$ map
the normalized load $\rho$ to power and QoS. Each surrogate is chosen by
cross-validation: Ridge regression where the response is close to linear, and
tree ensembles where saturation makes it nonlinear. Because the twin is the
evaluation environment for every controller, we check that these surrogates
reproduce the measured behavior on held-out data. The user-space power surrogate
reaches $R^2=0.99$, and its delay and loss surrogates reach $R^2$ between $0.97$
and $1.00$. DPDK power is almost constant across load (standard deviation about
$5$~mW from $0$ to $5$~Gbps), and the surrogate reproduces it to within $4$~mW,
so its low $R^2$ reflects this near-zero variance rather than a poor fit.
The same measurement traces also determine the activation durations of the two
realizations: $t^{\mathrm{act}}_{\mathrm{DPDK}}=24$~s and
$t^{\mathrm{act}}_{\mathrm{USR}}=3.3$~s. These measured values are important
because switching to DPDK is substantially more expensive than switching to the
user-space realization. The digital twin therefore captures both operating
operation and transition overhead. Live closed-loop validation is left for
future work; the present study focuses on controlled, repeatable evaluation
inside the calibrated twin.

\subsection{Traffic Traces and Prediction}

The traffic process is derived from the NetMob 2023 Lyon dataset, which provides city-scale mobile downlink demand over $100 \times 100$~m grid tiles at 15-minute resolution. We use these traces to represent the spatial and temporal variability of urban mobile traffic. Grid-level demand is first mapped to radio-site service areas using a Voronoi-based association constructed from base-station locations. The resulting site-level demand series are then grouped into $K=10$ heterogeneous MEC clusters, which define the traffic inputs of the digital twin.

To support proactive UPF orchestration, we train a cluster-level spatiotemporal graph neural network, denoted as ClusterSTGNN. The graph nodes correspond to the selected MEC clusters, and edges encode spatial neighborhood relations inherited from the underlying radio-site topology. The model uses a recurrent temporal encoder to capture recent traffic evolution and graph-attention layers to propagate information across neighboring clusters. Given 24 hours of history, the model predicts the traffic load over the next hour. The resulting forecasts are used as demand estimates within the digital twin.

\section{Multi-Site Control Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Observation and Objective}

Each agent observes only the local state of its own MEC site, enabling decentralized execution. At decision step $t$, the observation of agent $i$ contains 14 values: the current offered load $\lambda_{i,t}$, an 8-step load history
$\lambda_{i,t-1},\ldots,\lambda_{i,t-8}$, the one-step-ahead traffic forecast
$\hat{\lambda}_{i,t+1}$, the previously active UPF realization, the previous QoS score, the previous specific energy (W/Mbps), and a cooldown-progress signal that represents the fraction of the cooldown period that has elapsed since the last switch.

The fleet objective is the discounted sum of per-site rewards over an episode of length $T=1009$:
\begin{equation}
J(\pi) =
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^{t}
\sum_{i=1}^{K} r_{i,t}
\right],
\qquad \gamma = 0.995 .
\end{equation}
The sites are dynamically independent given their selected realizations, but they are coupled through the cooperative training objective.

\subsection{Reward Function}

The reward penalizes four quantities: operating  energy inefficiency, QoS degradation, switching energy, and rapid oscillation between realizations:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_{i,t} =
-\bigl(
\alpha\,\SECt_{i,t}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{QoS}}\max(0,\tau-Q_{i,t})
+
L^{\mathrm{SW}}_{i,t}
+
L^{\mathrm{CD}}_{i,t}
\bigr).
\end{equation}

The first term is based on operating specific energy consumption:
\begin{equation}
\SECt_{i,t}
=
\frac{P^{\mathrm{op}}_{i,t}}
{\max(\lambda_{i,t},\varepsilon)}
\quad [\mathrm{W/Mbps}],
\end{equation}
where $P^{\mathrm{op}}_{i,t}$ is the operating power of the active UPF realization at time $t$, excluding activation transients. This term rewards operating modes that carry traffic with lower power per Mbps.

The second term penalizes QoS degradation. The QoS score combines delay and packet-loss behavior:
\begin{equation}
Q_{i,t}
=
\min\bigl(
\mathrm{score}_{d}(d_{i,t}),
\mathrm{score}_{\ell}(\ell_{i,t})
\bigr).
\end{equation}
The delay and loss scores decrease linearly from 1, when the metric is within its budget, to 0, when the budget is exceeded by 100\%. The budgets are set from the measured testbed limits: 200~$\mu$s for delay and 5 packets per interval for loss. The threshold $\tau=0.9$ defines a safety margin.

The third term accounts for switching energy:
\begin{equation}
L^{\mathrm{SW}}_{i,t}
=
\lambda_{\mathrm{sw}} E^{\mathrm{sw}}_{i,t}.
\end{equation}
This penalty is applied only when the active realization changes. The activation energy $E^{\mathrm{sw}}_{i,t}$ is obtained from the digital twin using the measured activation behavior of the target realization. Since activating DPDK takes 24~s while activating the user-space realization takes 3.3~s, switching into DPDK is more expensive.

The final term, $L^{\mathrm{CD}}_{i,t}$, discourages rapid oscillation. It adds a soft surcharge when a site switches again before the four-step cooldown period has elapsed. The reward weights are
$\alpha=100$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{QoS}}=30$, $\tau=0.9$,
$\lambda_{\mathrm{sw}}=4.0$, with cooldown period 4 and cooldown cost 0.5.

\section{Cooperative MARL Controllers}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{MAPPO with shared actor and centralized critic}

The proposed controller uses MAPPO~\cite{ref:mappo}. Each agent acts through a
shared actor network that maps the 14-dimensional local site observation to a
binary action distribution over the two UPF realizations. The actor has two
hidden layers with 64 units each and 5,250 parameters. Because the same actor is
used by all MEC clusters, the execution policy remains decentralized while the
decision rule is learned from experience collected across the fleet.

Training uses a centralized critic. The critic receives the concatenation of the
ten local site observations, resulting in a 140-dimensional fleet-level input,
and outputs $K$ value estimates. It has two hidden layers with 128 units each
and 35,850 parameters. The critic is used only during training to estimate the
value function and improve credit assignment across sites.

Training follows PPO clipping~\cite{ref:ppo} with generalized advantage
estimation~\cite{ref:gae}, $\lambda=0.9$, per-agent advantage normalization over
both time and agents, and reward scaling of 0.01. The learning rate is
$3\times10^{-4}$, the entropy coefficient is 0.05, the rollout length is 1024,
the minibatch size is 256, and each update uses 10 epochs. Each run is trained
for 200k environment steps. The validation checkpoint with the best performance
is selected for testing, which avoids late-stage policy degradation observed in
some seeds.

\subsection{Independent PPO ensemble}

The IPPO reference trains ten independent PPO policies~\cite{ref:ippo}, one for
each MEC cluster. Each policy receives the same 14-dimensional local observation
used by the MAPPO actor, but learns only from its own cluster. The ensemble is
implemented with Stable-Baselines3~\cite{ref:sb3} and uses the same PPO
hyperparameters as MAPPO where applicable. During evaluation, the ten policies
are executed in parallel. This baseline tests whether the benefit of MAPPO comes
from shared learning and centralized value estimation rather than from PPO
itself.

\subsection{Centralized PPO}

The centralized reference uses a single PPO policy that observes the full
140-dimensional fleet state and outputs a joint action using a
$\mathrm{MultiDiscrete}([2]^K)$ action space. It receives the same reward and
training budget as the other learning-based controllers. This baseline tests
whether a fully centralized policy can solve the joint problem directly, without
parameter sharing or decentralized execution.

\subsection{Classical and static baselines}
The learned controllers are compared against non-learning references
evaluated on the same twin, traffic, and reward.

\emph{Always-DPDK} keeps every site in the high-performance realization at all times, it is the safe but inefficient static reference that current provisioning corresponds to.

\emph{Optimized hysteresis.} A per-site, decentralized two-threshold
controller that acts on the one-step forecast $\hat\lambda_{i,t+1}$,
exactly like the RL actors. Its operating points are read from the twin
rather than hand-tuned. A load sweep places the energy break-even at
91~Mbps and the USR QoS limit at 149~Mbps, so the upper threshold is their minimum less a 10~Mbps safety margin,
$t_{\mathrm{up}}=81$~Mbps. A site in USR switches to DPDK once
$\hat\lambda_{i,t+1}\!\ge\!t_{\mathrm{up}}$, and a site in DPDK switches
to USR only once
$\hat\lambda_{i,t+1}\!\le\!t_{\mathrm{down}}=t_{\mathrm{up}}-b$; the band
$b$ between the two thresholds suppresses oscillation, and after any
switch the realization is locked for a four-step cooldown. We call the
controller \emph{optimized} for two reasons. First, its thresholds
follow from measured twin physics rather than the manual analysis used
in our earlier work~\cite{ref:slices}. Second, we sweep
$b$ and report the best-performing operating
point ($b=50$~Mbps). Together these give the classical family its
strongest form, so the learned controllers are measured against a
well-tuned competitor.


\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
All trained controllers are evaluated on the held-out test slice using
deterministic actions and a fixed reset seed. MAPPO and IPPO are trained over
eight seeds and centralized PPO is trained over four
seeds because its failure mode is consistent and large in magnitude. The
reported metrics are total reward over all clusters and time steps, total fleet
energy in watt-hours, QoS-violation rate, user-space usage share, and switch
count.
\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Overall comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:headline} summarizes the test-slice results. MAPPO obtains the
best reward among all evaluated controllers. Relative to the strongest
hysteresis configuration, it improves reward by 9.7\%. Relative to always-DPDK,
it improves reward by 21.5\%, and relative to centralized PPO by 33.8\%. These
values form the 10--34\% improvement range reported in the abstract. MAPPO also
has the lowest energy among the trained policies, with 1966~Wh compared with
1973~Wh for IPPO and 2071~Wh for always-DPDK. The energy difference between
MAPPO and IPPO is small, but MAPPO achieves better reward because its user-space
usage is placed more safely, leading to fewer QoS penalties. MAPPO is also the
most active controller (Table~\ref{tab:headline}), yet its switches are well
placed: at a comparable user-space share it attains the lowest energy and
QoS-violation rate among the learned controllers, so the additional switching is
repaid in reward. Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} recasts this comparison as an
energy--QoS trade-off: MAPPO occupies the efficient corner, dominating the IPPO
ensemble, hysteresis, and centralized PPO on both axes, while static always-DPDK
is the only non-dominated alternative---avoiding violations entirely but at a
${\sim}5\%$ energy premium.

\subsection{Significance of the MAPPO--IPPO difference}
Although the MAPPO advantage over IPPO is smaller than its advantage over
hysteresis or centralized PPO, it is statistically robust. A paired bootstrap
over the eight seed-matched MAPPO-minus-IPPO reward differences, using 10,000
resamples, gives a mean gain of 157 reward units, a 95\% confidence interval of
$[+39,+271]$, and a one-sided $p$-value of 0.004. Seven of the eight paired
seeds favor MAPPO. The only IPPO win occurs at seed 23, where MAPPO selects the
user-space realization more aggressively and incurs a slightly higher QoS
violation rate. The bootstrap distribution and per-seed comparison are shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:bootstrap}.

\subsection{Per-cluster behavior and transfer across sites}
The per-cluster analysis in Fig.~\ref{fig:percluster} explains where the MAPPO
gain originates. MAPPO outperforms IPPO on seven of the ten clusters, matches it
on a high-load DPDK-bound cluster, and loses slightly on two mid-load clusters.
The strongest gains occur on low- and mid-load clusters, including c5, c0, c7,
and c4. These are precisely the sites where the user-space realization can be
beneficial but where an independent local learner may not observe enough
informative transitions to confidently use it.

This pattern is consistent with cross-site transfer through the shared actor.
During training, MAPPO observes low-load operating conditions across several
clusters and learns a common decision rule for safe user-space usage. That rule
can then be applied at sites whose own local trace contains less evidence. In
contrast, IPPO must learn each site independently and is therefore more likely
to stay close to the conservative DPDK behavior on data-poor clusters. On the
highest-load clusters, where DPDK is almost always required, MAPPO correctly
avoids excessive user-space usage.

\subsection{Failure mode of centralized PPO}
Centralized PPO performs substantially worse than all other controllers. Its
average reward is $-8542$, with high seed-to-seed variability. The main failure mode is unsafe
assignment of the user-space realization to one or two clusters for long
periods. In some seeds, individual clusters experience very high QoS-violation
rates because the centralized policy does not learn the per-site structure of
the problem within the available budget.

This result is important because it shows that more centralized observation does
not automatically produce better control. The joint state has 140 dimensions and
the joint binary action space grows as $2^K$. A shared decentralized actor, by
contrast, solves the same local 14-to-2 mapping at each site while using a
centralized critic only during training. The comparison therefore supports the
main architectural conclusion: cooperative structure must be introduced in a way
that preserves the local decision structure of the UPF-control problem.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper studied energy-aware UPF realization selection across a heterogeneous
fleet of MEC clusters. The problem arises because DPDK-based UPFs provide strong
high-load performance but carry a high idle-power cost, while user-space UPFs
are more energy-efficient at low load but have a narrower safe operating range.
Runtime selection between these realizations can reduce energy consumption, but
it must be robust to traffic variation, forecast error, QoS constraints, and
measured switching overheads.

We formulated the fleet-level problem as cooperative multi-agent reinforcement
learning and evaluated it using a measurement-grounded digital twin calibrated
from a physical UPF testbed. The twin incorporates per-realization power and QoS
surrogates and measured activation times, allowing switching energy to be
included directly in specific energy consumption. Under this evaluation setup,
MAPPO with a shared actor and centralized critic outperformed the strongest
swept hysteresis baseline, an IPPO ensemble, and centralized PPO by 10--34\% in
reward. The MAPPO advantage over IPPO was statistically significant and was
concentrated on low-load clusters, suggesting that shared policies transfer
useful behavior from data-rich to data-poor sites.

The results show that coordination architecture is central to energy-aware UPF
orchestration. A fully centralized PPO policy fails to exploit the structure of
the problem, whereas a shared decentralized actor combined with centralized
training provides both transfer and scalability. Future work will evaluate
zero-shot transfer to unseen MEC clusters, incorporate shared-resource coupling
among sites, and validate the learned policies in a live closed-loop UPF
deployment.
